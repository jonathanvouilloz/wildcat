Here's the fast answer: the **10,000 THB embassy fee** is never refundable, rejected or not. Course and training fees are a different story entirely, because they're set by whoever runs the course, not by immigration.

I'm Meaw. I own and run **Wildcat Muay Thai** in **Chiang Mai**, and I prepare soft power documents for DTV applicants myself, for free, over WhatsApp. The question I hear most often, more than anything about eligibility or paperwork, is some version of "what happens to my money if I get rejected." It's the single biggest source of frustration I see in applicant communities, and most of it comes from mixing up two very different fees. If you want the full picture first, [the real cost breakdown of the whole DTV visa](/blog/dtv-visa-cost-breakdown) covers every layer, embassy, course, and agent. This article sticks to one question: what's refundable if your **DTV visa** gets rejected, and what to check before you pay anyone.

## The embassy fee is never refundable, whatever happens

The **10,000 THB visa fee** you pay on the official e-Visa portal is gone the moment you submit, approved or not. This is an immigration rule, not a policy any provider sets, which means nobody, not Wildcat, not an agent, not the embassy itself, can negotiate it back for you.

That fee is paid directly through the [official Thailand e-Visa portal](https://thaievisa.go.th), the only system the Thai government runs for DTV applications. Once it's submitted, it's processed against your file whether the outcome is a yes or a no. If your application is rejected, you don't get a second attempt at that same payment: a new submission means a new 10,000 THB fee.

This is the fact that catches people off guard, and it's worth saying plainly: **is the DTV fee refundable**? No, never, regardless of why you were rejected. According to [The Thaiger's DTV visa guide](https://thethaiger.com/guides/visa-information/thailand-digital-nomad-visa), "the fee is non-refundable regardless of outcome." One applicant in a DTV forum described being told the same thing directly by an agent, and called it frustrating. That reaction is fair. It's also exactly why the fee should be the last thing you pay, not the first.

## Course and training fees: it depends on the provider

Unlike the embassy fee, your soft power course fee (Muay Thai or otherwise) is set entirely by whoever runs the course, and so is the refund policy attached to it. Course prices typically run **7,500 to 25,000 THB** depending on length and provider, according to [our layer-by-layer look at every DTV fee](/blog/dtv-visa-cost-breakdown), and that entire range comes with a different refund answer at every provider. This is the part most generic visa guides skip, and it's the part that actually varies.

Some course providers publicly offer a full refund if the application isn't accepted, and that's worth asking about directly before you commit any money. Others don't offer refunds at all, or only a partial one. Neither approach is wrong on its own. The problem is applicants who assume a refund policy exists, pay first, and only find out otherwise after a rejection.

At Wildcat, we don't currently publish a fixed refund percentage, because we'd rather be direct about it than promise a number we can't guarantee case by case. If you're weighing a soft power course anywhere, **ask us directly, we're clear about what's included**, and expect the same clarity from any provider before you pay them a single baht.

## Common reasons a DTV application gets rejected

Most DTV rejections come down to a handful of documented, avoidable issues rather than bad luck. Understanding them upfront is the best way to reduce your risk before you ever pay an embassy fee.

The most common reasons include:

- **Insufficient or poorly documented funds.** The 500,000 THB threshold has to sit in a personal bank account under the applicant's own name, not a business account, brokerage account, or crypto wallet.
- **An incomplete or inconsistent soft power file.** Immigration wants to see a coherent story: the course length, the provider's documentation, and your declared plans should all line up.
- **Missing or expired documents.** A passport nearing expiry, an outdated bank statement, or a course confirmation letter that doesn't match your application dates.
- **A mismatch between your stated plan and your paperwork.** If your file says one thing and your documents say another, that inconsistency is a red flag reviewers are trained to catch.

None of this means a high percentage of applications get rejected, and no honest source publishes a reliable rejection rate. It just means these are the documented failure points worth checking twice, on your own file, before you submit.

## How to reduce your risk before you pay anyone

The single most useful thing you can do is ask about a refund policy before you pay for a course, not after you've already been rejected. That one question, asked early, avoids most of the frustration described in the previous sections.

Beyond that, a few concrete steps lower your risk meaningfully:

1. **Check your funds first.** Confirm your 500,000 THB sits in a personal account under your name, well before you touch a course fee or an embassy payment. If you're unsure which assets qualify, [our breakdown of what counts as proof of funds](/blog/dtv-visa-proof-of-funds) covers bank accounts, stocks, and crypto in detail.
2. **Get your soft power documentation right.** Follow [the complete step-by-step application guide](/dtv-visa/how-to-apply) so your course paperwork and your declared plan actually match.
3. **Ask the refund question in writing.** A provider's answer, or lack of one, tells you a lot about how they'll handle things if your file doesn't go through.
4. **Pay the embassy fee last.** Once your funds and your soft power file are solid, the 10,000 THB payment is the final step, not the first one.

No provider, agent, or camp can guarantee approval, and any that claims otherwise is a warning sign in itself. I've watched applicants stress over the wrong things: the passport photo, the exact wording of a cover letter. What actually matters is the funds and the file. Get those two right, and the rest tends to sort itself out.

## FAQ on the DTV visa refund policy

### Is the DTV visa embassy fee refundable if I'm rejected?

No. The 10,000 THB embassy fee is non-refundable whether your DTV visa application is approved or rejected. This is an immigration rule, not something any provider or agent can change.

### Are course or training fees refundable if my DTV is rejected?

It depends entirely on the provider. Some course providers publicly offer a full refund if the application isn't accepted, while others offer partial refunds or none at all. Always ask before you pay, since immigration has no rule covering this.

### What are the most common reasons a DTV application is rejected?

Insufficient or poorly documented funds, an incomplete or inconsistent soft power file, missing or expired documents, and a mismatch between your declared plan and your paperwork are the most commonly reported reasons.

### Should I ask about refund policy before paying for a soft power course?

Yes. Asking a provider for their refund policy in writing before you pay is the single most effective way to avoid losing money on a course fee if your DTV visa application doesn't go through.
