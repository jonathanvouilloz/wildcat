Short answer: yes, it can hurt, and no, that does not make it reckless. **Whether Muay Thai is dangerous** depends on how you train, not just on the sport itself. It is a full-contact sport, so you will collect bruises, and every so often something worse. The good news is that the size of the risk is mostly in your hands: how hard you spar, how quickly you ramp up, and whether a coach is watching.

We run Wildcat in Chiang Mai and teach beginners every week, so this is the honest version we would give a friend. It is about training safety. If you are wondering [how effective Muay Thai is in a real street situation](/blog/muay-thai-self-defense), that is a different question with its own article.

*This article is general information, not medical advice. If you have a health condition, an old injury or a concern about your head, neck or heart, talk to a doctor before you start.*

## Is Muay Thai dangerous? The short answer

Think of it like any contact sport. Football, rugby and judo all have injuries. Muay Thai adds shins, elbows and knees to the mix, so the impacts are real.

What the research says, in plain terms:

- Most reported injuries are minor: bruises, cuts, sprains and strains.
- Serious injuries exist, and they cluster around competition and hard sparring, not around a first pad class.
- Nobody can give you a single "danger score", because studies measure different groups (beginners, amateurs, professional fighters) in different ways.

So the useful question is not "is it dangerous?" but "how dangerous is the way this gym trains?" The rest of this article gives you the tools to judge that.

## What actually gets hurt: the real injuries

Let's look at the evidence for **Muay Thai injuries**, starting with the study that is cited most often.

A team from Manchester interviewed 152 people who trained or fought in Muay Thai in UK gyms and at a Muay Thai gala in the Netherlands: 19 beginners, 82 amateurs and 51 professionals. Injuries to the lower body were the most common in every group. Head injuries came second for amateurs and professionals, and trunk injuries came second for beginners. Soft tissue damage, meaning bruises and similar, was the most common type at every level, followed by fractures for professionals and by sprains and strains for beginners and amateurs. Only 7% of beginners' injuries kept them out of training for a week or more, against 4% for amateurs and 5.8% for professionals ([Gartland et al., British Journal of Sports Medicine, 2001](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11579062/)). It is an old study with a small group, but the pattern still matches what we see: shins, feet, hands and bruised thighs.

Other studies point the same way:

- A survey of 195 Muay Thai fighters found that among those hurt in their latest fight, the arms and legs were the main body region injured, at 58% ([Strotmeyer et al., Injury Epidemiology, 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27917452/)).
- A 2025 study followed 663 athletes in official championship bouts. 91.4% had no health issues afterwards. The most frequent injuries were nosebleeds (about 2%), concussion (about 1.5%), rib trauma and soft tissue strains, and about 10% of athletes received some medical treatment ([Hallaçeli et al., Injury Epidemiology, 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40420121/)).
- Because Muay Thai is trained barefoot, feet take a beating. A survey of 123 kickboxers in northern Thailand found calluses in 59%, toe deformities in 49.3%, and a link between toe deformities and long weekly training hours ([Vaseenon et al., Journal of the Medical Association of Thailand, 2015](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25775734/)).
- Teeth and jaw matter too. In a survey of 260 Thai boxing athletes in northeastern Thailand, 23.5% had dental or jaw injuries ([Chatrchaiwiwatana et al., Journal of the Medical Association of Thailand, 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29906017/)). A mouthguard is cheap next to a dentist.

Notice what these numbers do not tell you: how likely a casual, twice-a-week beginner is to get hurt. Most of these groups are competitors, and competitors hit harder and more often than you will in a first month.

In practice, the things that go wrong for beginners are usually ordinary: sore shins, a bruised thigh, a jammed toe, a tweaked ankle or a strained muscle from doing too much too soon.

## Sparring, head impacts and brain health

Sparring is where risk goes up, so it deserves its own honest section.

**Is sparring compulsory?** In a well-run gym, no beginner should be pushed into hard sparring. Sparring is normally introduced gradually, kept light while you learn, and chosen by you. We cannot speak for every gym, so ask directly. As for Wildcat, message us and ask, and you will get a straight answer.

**What about head impacts?** This is the part to take seriously. Strikes to the head are part of the sport, and researchers describe Muay Thai as a full-contact sport with a high incidence of head trauma among competitors ([Lystad and Strotmeyer, Injury Epidemiology, 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29888376/)). A few things worth knowing:

- In a survey of 105 combat sport athletes and coaches, 68.5% of coaches said health-care professionals were probably absent during training, and athletes mostly relied on self-diagnosis to decide whether they had a concussion. Only 5.7% of coaches correctly recognized that a concussion is a form of traumatic brain injury ([Follmer et al., The Physician and Sportsmedicine, 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32067547/)). Knowledge gaps are common, so your gym's habits matter.
- In another survey of 193 competitive Muay Thai athletes, about 69% said they were likely to report concussion symptoms. The authors saw room for improvement ([Lystad and Strotmeyer, 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29888376/)).
- Small studies have found measurable differences in balance and eye-movement tests between active combat sport athletes and non-fighters. In one of them, 45% of 40 athletes had an abnormal score on at least one test, against 22.5% of controls ([Brown et al., Brain Injury, 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35943357/)). Another found delayed balance reflexes in boxers and Muay Thai fighters ([Banman et al., Journal of Neurotrauma, 2021](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33752455/)). These are exploratory studies of people who fight, so they do not tell you what happens to a recreational trainee.

The honest summary: repeated hits to the head carry a risk, the long-term picture is still being studied, and nobody can promise you zero risk. What lowers the odds is boring and effective:

- Light, controlled sparring, and none at all until you feel ready.
- Head contact kept out of sparring while you are new.
- A partner who matches your level and a coach who steps in.
- A clear rule that a knock to the head means you stop for the day.
- Reporting symptoms honestly (headache, dizziness, confusion, nausea) and seeing a doctor if they do not settle.

If you have had concussions before, tell your coach and your doctor. You can do a lot of Muay Thai with no head contact at all: pad work, bag work, technique and fitness.

## Beginners: how a first class keeps risk low

Here is the good news for **Muay Thai for beginners**: a first class is not a fight. Most people picture a ring and a bell, and what you actually do is closer to a workout with technique.

A typical beginner class follows a simple shape: warm-up, a few basic moves, pad work, cool-down. That is what we describe on our beginner page, and it is the standard to expect from any good gym. You can read [what a first class looks like](/classes/beginners) here, including what to bring.

Why this keeps risk low:

- You practise on pads and bags, not on a moving opponent.
- Your coach controls the pace, and you learn to relax before you learn to hit hard.
- You learn to defend as early as you learn to attack.
- You can, and should, say so if something hurts.

You also need the right kit. Mouthguard and shin protection are worth having as you progress, and wraps protect your hands and wrists. [The gear beginners actually need](/blog/muay-thai-gear-beginners) is a short list.

### Red flags in any gym

Before you pay, watch a session and ask questions. Walk away, or at least think twice, if you see:

- Hard sparring in a beginner's first week.
- A coach who laughs off a head knock or tells people to "walk it off".
- No warm-up, or people being pushed to spar tired.
- Nobody asking about your injuries, age or health.
- An "everyone fights" attitude with no choice.

### Questions to ask before you sign up

1. When do beginners start sparring, and is it optional?
2. How light is the sparring, and is head contact allowed?
3. What happens if someone gets a knock to the head?
4. Who supervises, and how many students does each coach handle?
5. Is protective gear required, and what can I borrow?

A gym that answers these calmly is a good sign. A gym that gets defensive is telling you something too.

## Age, kids and women: who should think twice

**Too old to start?** There is no single cut-off. Plenty of people start Muay Thai at 30, 40 or 50, and a good coach adjusts the class to you: lighter pad work, more warm-up, fewer hard rounds, no sparring. If you have joint problems, heart or blood pressure issues, or are recovering from an injury, see a doctor first. That is not because Muay Thai is forbidden at your age, but because your doctor knows your body and we do not.

**Kids.** Kids' training can be very different from adult training, and a good coach keeps young students on technique and games rather than hard contact. We have not published a minimum age for our classes, so message us before you book if you are thinking of a child. Separately, Thailand has a real public debate about child fighters in competition. It flared in 2018 after 13-year-old Anucha Tasako died of a brain hemorrhage following a bout near Bangkok, and lawmakers discussed a ban on competitive fights for children under 12 ([Al Jazeera, 14 November 2018](https://www.aljazeera.com/sports/2018/11/14/child-kickboxing-debate-heats-up-in-thailand-after-teen-killed)). That debate is about competition, and it is not the same thing as a kids' class.

**Women.** The risks are the same as for everyone else, and the choice comes down to what you enjoy and how you like to train. If you want to know what a class is like, our guide to [Muay Thai for women](/blog/muay-thai-for-women) covers it in detail.

## FAQ: Muay Thai risks, answered

### Is Muay Thai more dangerous than boxing?

We cannot give you an honest "yes" or "no". Studies measure different people in different ways, so ranking sports is unreliable. The difference in what gets hit is real: Muay Thai adds kicks, knees and elbows, and boxing is fists only. Your risk depends far more on how you train than on which name is on the door.

### Is Muay Thai more dangerous than BJJ or MMA?

Each has its own injury pattern: striking arts carry more impacts, grappling arts carry more joint and rib injuries. For a side-by-side look, see [Muay Thai vs BJJ](/blog/muay-thai-vs-bjj).

### Is Muay Thai safe for beginners?

It can be, in a gym that keeps beginners on technique and pads, controls the pace and makes sparring optional. Many beginners come away with sore shins and tired muscles rather than anything serious. See the section above for questions to ask.

### Why do my shins hurt?

Sore shins are common at the start and usually mean your legs are adapting to impact. Rest, build up slowly and follow your coach's advice. Sharp pain, swelling that does not settle, or pain that gets worse with rest is a reason to see a doctor.

### Can you get knocked out in training?

It can happen, mainly in hard sparring. That is why control and supervision matter, and why light sparring is the standard for new students. If someone is knocked out, they need medical attention, and they should not train again until they have been checked.

### Can Muay Thai kill you?

Very serious harm is rare in the studies above, which mostly describe minor injuries, but the sport is not risk free and nobody can promise otherwise, which is exactly why supervision, protection and honest coaching are worth paying for.

### Do I need travel insurance to train in Thailand?

Read your policy. Many policies exclude or limit contact sports, and some cover only certain activities. Check the exclusions and ask your insurer before you go, especially for a longer stay.

## Conclusion

Muay Thai is a contact sport with real risks, and pretending otherwise would not help you. But the risks are not random. They rise with hard sparring, fast progression and poor supervision, and they fall with light, controlled training, good gear, honest coaches and rest. Head knocks deserve extra respect. Everything else is usually bruises and sore shins.
