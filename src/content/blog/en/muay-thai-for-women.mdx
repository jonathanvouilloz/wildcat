Someone asked me last week: *"Is muay thai okay for women? I've always wanted to try but the gyms I've seen look so intense."*

I smiled, because I run the camp. My name is Meaw. I grew up in Chiang Mai, I've trained muay thai most of my life, and about eight years ago I opened Wildcat for exactly that reason: an open-air place where serious training and a welcoming atmosphere live side by side. Most mornings, roughly half the people hitting pads in our ring are women. Different countries, different fitness levels, different ages. The question "is it okay for women?" has never really come up here, because the answer is obvious when you look around.

But I understand why you ask. I want to answer it properly.

## What actually happens in your first class

You arrive. Someone shows you where to leave your shoes and hands you gloves and wraps, both free to borrow. Your coach takes you through the basics: how to stand, how to move your feet, how to hold your hands. It looks simple from the outside. It takes real concentration when you're doing it.

The first session is mostly footwork and a few pad rounds. You work at your own pace. Nobody puts you in front of an experienced fighter and tells you to spar. That doesn't happen here, full stop. Sparring is something you opt into when you feel ready, usually after a few weeks of technique work.

The session lasts about an hour. You will be tired in a way that feels clean rather than destroyed. Then breakfast, if you time it right, because there's a little place next to the garden where people eat together after training.

That's it. That's the first class.

## The physical changes people don't talk about enough

Muay thai builds strength through the entire body, but not in the way a gym machine does. Kicks come from the hip. The teep (front push kick) trains your core to brace and release explosively. Defensive movement builds hip stability and ankle strength that most training methods barely touch.

Most women who train for a month report the same things: the upper body gets noticeably stronger (pad work and clinch work require real shoulder and back engagement), posture improves, and the lower body changes shape in ways that running doesn't replicate.

Here's what I find interesting about muay thai for women specifically: every technique chains your whole body together. A roundhouse kick isn't a quad exercise. It's a full rotation from the ball of your foot through your hip, your core, your shoulder. Your body learns to move as a connected system, which is quite different from isolated weight training.

Before training, I had clients who said they wanted "cardio that doesn't bore them." After a month, they were not thinking about cardio at all. They were thinking about whether their guard drops too early and how to fix it.

## What muay thai camp training is really like as a woman

This is the part the YouTube videos don't show well.

Training at a camp is different from training at a city gym. At a city gym you put your earphones in, do your session, and leave. At a camp there's a community around the sessions. You see the same people every morning. You notice when someone's timing improves. People cheer genuinely.

At Wildcat specifically, the outdoor setting changes the atmosphere completely. We train in an open-air ring surrounded by trees. Natural light in the mornings, a cool breeze in the evenings. It does not feel like a cage. It feels like a garden where people happen to be hitting things.

I've had women tell me they were nervous before the first session and surprised by how quickly that went away. One student from France, who came for two weeks and ended up staying six, said it felt like the most supported she'd ever been in a physical activity. Not because anyone was easy on her (the coaches push you) but because the pushing felt personal rather than generic.

Small camps do that. Big gyms mostly can't.

## What to wear to your first session

Keep it simple. Here's the actual list:

- Shorts or leggings that you can kick in (no jeans, nothing that restricts at the hip)
- A fitted top or sports bra with a layer over it if you prefer
- Bare feet or flip flops you can slip off at the ring

That's genuinely it. You don't need to buy anything before you come. Gloves and hand wraps are available to borrow at the camp. A lot of women decide to buy their own wraps after a few sessions because you develop a preference for how they feel, but there's no pressure and no minimum purchase.

If you want to wear a hijab or any head covering, that's fine. If you want to cover your legs, no one will comment. The rule here: wear what lets you move and keeps you cool in a tropical climate.

## The intimidating gym problem

I want to address this directly because I hear it often.

The muay thai gyms that look intimidating in photos are usually fight camps. They train fighters preparing for professional bouts. The energy is different, the schedule is different, and if you walked in off the street with no experience, you would feel out of place. That's accurate. You are out of place.

That's not where most people should start, and it's not what most camps actually are.

A training camp that welcomes travelers and longer-stay students is a completely different setup. The goal isn't to produce fighters (though fighters come here too). The goal is to offer serious training to anyone who wants it, and "serious" and "welcoming" are not opposites.

The garden ring here has seen complete beginners on their first-ever day standing two meters from students who've been training for years. Nobody is watching you with judgment. They were that beginner not long ago.

## How long before you see results

Honestly, the physical changes take a few weeks to appear clearly, but the *feeling* changes faster. After your third or fourth session, the footwork starts to click a little. You stop thinking about your feet separately from your hands. The movement pattern starts to feel like yours.

What women consistently mention after a month: functional strength that carries into daily life, better posture from all the shoulder and back work, and a quiet kind of groundedness that's hard to describe but I hear it often enough that I trust it's real.

The ones who come back the following year are not coming back for their abs. They're coming back for the morning sessions, the people, the way it makes them feel in their own body.
