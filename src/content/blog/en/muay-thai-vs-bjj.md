Should you learn striking or grappling? It is one of the questions we hear most at the camp, and the honest answer starts with a disclosure: Wildcat teaches Muay Thai, and we do not teach BJJ. So you should expect us to lean toward our own art. To make up for that, this guide says out loud where jiu jitsu does better, and it ends with a verdict by profile instead of a trophy for one side.

If you are still choosing between two striking arts, we covered that separately in [Muay Thai vs kickboxing](/blog/muay-thai-vs-kickboxing). Here the question is bigger: standing up or on the ground?

## Muay Thai vs BJJ in one table

The short version: **Muay Thai vs jiu jitsu** is a choice between hitting and holding. Muay Thai is a stand-up art where you strike and clinch. Brazilian jiu jitsu (BJJ) is mostly about taking the fight to the ground and controlling or submitting your partner there.

| | Muay Thai | BJJ |
|---|---|---|
| Main job | Strike and clinch on your feet | Control and submit on the ground |
| Tools | Fists, elbows, knees, shins | Positions, escapes, chokes, joint locks |
| Distance | Long to close range, plus the clinch | Body to body |
| Live practice | Pad work, then light sparring | Live rolling with a partner |
| Typical wear and tear | Shins, forearms, bruising | Knees, ribs, shoulders, fingers |
| What you feel after 3 months | Basic combos, balance, a solid teep and roundhouse | Basic escapes, positions and a first submission or two |
| Getting started | Gloves and wraps can be borrowed at Wildcat | Usually a gi or no-gi kit, depending on the school |

A word on the "3 months" row: it is a general picture, not a promise. How fast you progress depends on how often you train, your body and your coach.

## Striking vs grappling: what each one teaches you

**Muay Thai** is a Thai martial art and combat sport that uses stand-up striking and clinching. Its nickname, the "Art of Eight Limbs", refers to the combined use of fists, elbows, knees and shins, according to [Wikipedia's Muay Thai overview](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muay_Thai). In practice, you learn to manage distance, kick with your shins, use your knees and elbows up close, and control a person in the clinch. Balance is the quiet skill under all of it. If you want to see where that starts, our guide to [the Muay Thai stance](/blog/muay-thai-stance-basics) shows the first thing every beginner works on.

**BJJ** is described as "primarily a ground-based fighting style" that involves taking an opponent to the ground, gaining a dominant position, and using joint locks or chokes to force a submission, according to [Wikipedia's overview of Brazilian jiu-jitsu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brazilian_jiu-jitsu). It grew out of Japanese judo taught in Brazil in the early 1900s and was developed by the Gracie family from around 1925. Its core idea is that a smaller person can defend against a bigger one by using leverage and weight distribution. Rolling, the live sparring that gives BJJ its rhythm, plays a big role from early on.

They feel very different in the body. Muay Thai is explosive, rhythmic and often loud: pads, kicks, breath. BJJ is slower, more like a physical puzzle, and you spend a lot of time in contact with your partner. Some people love the first and hate the second, and that gut reaction is a useful signal.

## Which is better for self-defense?

People search **whether BJJ is good for self-defense** and whether Muay Thai is, usually at the same time. Our answer: each one is good at something the other is not, and the "best" one depends on what goes wrong.

What Muay Thai gives you:

- The ability to hit hard, on purpose, from a distance and up close.
- Comfort with being hit, which changes how you react under pressure.
- A way to stay on your feet and create space to leave.

Where Muay Thai runs out: it has little to say once a fight goes to the floor, and it does not teach you to deal with weapons or several attackers by itself.

What BJJ gives you:

- A real answer to being taken down or held on the ground.
- Control of a bigger person without needing to strike.
- Regular live practice against a resisting partner.

Where BJJ runs out: it is mostly a ground art, so you are less prepared for strikes coming in, and going to the ground in a real situation can be risky, especially with more than one person involved.

That is why the debate about Muay Thai vs BJJ in a street fight never ends. A one-on-one fight on a soft mat and a real street are very different, and the winner in a video does not tell you what to train. Our own opinion, not a study: if self-defense is your main reason, the two arts cover each other's gaps, and that is why so many people end up doing both. If you can only pick one to start, choose the one you will keep attending. Consistency beats the "right" art on paper.

For the street-specific detail, including what a ring skill does and does not transfer, read [our full self-defense breakdown](/blog/muay-thai-self-defense). It also has a shorter Muay Thai vs BJJ section from the self-defense angle. This article is about picking a discipline; that one is about being safer.

## Injuries and training load

Both arts carry a real risk of getting hurt. We are not going to tell you one is safer, because the research does not support that, and studies use different methods and different groups of people.

Here is what we could verify:

- A 2024 integrative systematic review in the Revista Brasileira de Ortopedia looked at seven studies covering 2,847 jiu-jitsu practitioners. It found a high prevalence of musculoskeletal injuries, most often in the knee, the chest and rib area, followed by the shoulder. Injuries were more common during training, in men over 30, and in beginners.
- For Muay Thai, a 2016 survey of 195 fighters in Injury Epidemiology found that among those hurt in their latest fight, the extremities (arms and legs) were the main body region injured, at 58%, compared with the head. Those are fighters who compete, though, and most readers of this article will not.
- A 2025 study of 663 athletes in official Muay Thai championship bouts found that 91.4% had no health issues after their fights. The most frequent injuries were nosebleeds, concussion, rib trauma and soft tissue strains, at low rates.

Notice what these numbers do not tell you: how likely a casual, twice-a-week beginner is to get hurt. Most of the risk in both arts comes from how hard you spar and how fast you ramp up. In a typical beginner class you are not fighting anyone. You are learning to move.

Practical habits that help in either art: warm up properly, tell your coach about old injuries, tap early in BJJ, keep sparring light while you are new, and rest when something hurts in a sharp or joint-related way. This is general information, not medical advice. If you have a knee, shoulder or neck problem, talk to a doctor before choosing.

## For women, kids, older beginners and busy schedules

**Women.** Both arts work well for women, and the choice comes down to the same things as for anyone else: what feels fun, what your goal is, and how you like to train. Some women prefer BJJ because the core idea is using leverage against size. Others prefer Muay Thai because striking feels empowering and the fitness side is intense. If you want the full picture of what a first month looks like, see [Muay Thai for women](/blog/muay-thai-for-women). On the question of Muay Thai vs BJJ for women in particular, we would not push you either way. Try a class of each if you can.

**Kids and teens.** Both arts exist for young people, and a good coach adapts the training to age. We do not publish a minimum age for our own classes here, so message us before you book if you are thinking of a child. For any art, look for a coach who keeps young students on technique and games, not hard contact.

**Beginners in their 30s and 40s.** You are not too old, but you should be patient. The BJJ review above noted more injuries in men over 30 and in beginners, so a slow start matters. Muay Thai suits you if you like fitness and rhythm; BJJ suits you if you like problem-solving and slower, closer work. Whichever you pick, keep sessions short and sparring light for the first months.

**Busy schedules.** Two sessions a week of either art will teach you something real. What does not work is a burst of five sessions in one week followed by a month of nothing. If you are travelling in Thailand, a few weeks of regular Muay Thai is an easy way to build the habit. BJJ that fits a short stay can be harder to line up, but Chiang Mai does have BJJ academies, so it is worth looking.

## Can you train both, and how to pick a verdict by profile

Yes, and many people do. The simplest order is to start with one art for two or three months, so the movements feel familiar, then add the second at a lower frequency. Doing both from day one is possible, but your body will feel it: too much new skill and too many new aches at the same time. Give yourself at least one rest day between hard sessions.

Here is our verdict by profile:

- **You want fitness, energy and a strong stand-up game:** start with Muay Thai.
- **You want self-defense and are willing to train both:** Muay Thai first for standing skills, BJJ second for the ground.
- **You like calm, technical, puzzle-like training:** start with BJJ.
- **You are in Thailand for a few weeks:** Muay Thai, because it is the local art and easy to find. Book [a first Muay Thai class](/classes/beginners) and see how it feels.
- **You have a knee, shoulder or neck history:** talk to a doctor first, and go slow with either art.
- **Tight budget:** the cheapest art is the one you can borrow gear for and attend often. Check [our class schedule and prices](/classes) if you are considering Wildcat.

Remember that we do not teach BJJ, so if the ground is what you want, look for a BJJ academy near you or in Chiang Mai and ask for a trial class. And if you are unsure, the quickest test is the cheapest one: try a session of each and notice which you look forward to.

## FAQ

### Who would win, Muay Thai or BJJ?

It depends on the rules, the size of the two people and the situation. In a stand-up fight, the striker has the advantage. Once it goes to the ground, the grappler does. This is why mixed martial arts teach both, and why the question is less useful than "which one should I train for my goals?"

### Muay Thai vs BJJ vs boxing: how do they compare?

Boxing is the narrowest of the three: fists only, standing. Muay Thai adds elbows, knees, shins and the clinch. BJJ is the ground specialist. Boxing is a good pick if you want fast hands and simple training, but it does not cover kicks, clinch or the ground.

### Is Muay Thai or BJJ better for beginners?

Neither is easier in every way. Muay Thai has an easy entry: you learn a few strikes and start moving. BJJ can feel more confusing at first because positions have many names and steps. Choose based on what you enjoy.

### Can I do BJJ at Wildcat?

No. Wildcat teaches Muay Thai only. We are glad to tell you what a class is like so you can compare it with a BJJ trial class elsewhere.

## Conclusion

Muay Thai and BJJ are two answers to two different problems: what to do standing, and what to do on the ground. Neither wins on paper. The best choice is the one that fits your goal, your body and your schedule, and plenty of people end up learning both.
