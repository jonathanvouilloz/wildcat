A **personal bank account with 500,000 THB** is the safest, most universally accepted way to prove funds for the DTV visa. Other assets, stocks, crypto, several accounts combined, get much less consistent answers, and some of what's published online actually contradicts itself on this exact point.

I'm Meaw. I own and run Wildcat Muay Thai in Chiang Mai, and I talk to DTV candidates every week who assume their **proof of funds** situation is straightforward until it isn't. I've seen someone get told their brokerage account was fine, only to find out later it wasn't, and someone else nearly convert crypto at the worst possible moment because nobody had explained the rule clearly. This article goes asset by asset: bank account, stocks, crypto, multiple accounts, and borrowed funds, with an honest note about where the answer genuinely isn't settled. If you haven't checked the basics yet, start with [the full DTV eligibility guide](/dtv-visa/eligibility); if you're budgeting the whole application, see [the real cost breakdown of the whole DTV visa](/blog/dtv-visa-cost-breakdown). The 500,000 THB rule itself hasn't moved, but two separate conditions were added on 31 August 2026, and they're worth reading before you plan your timeline: [the new DTV rules from 31 August 2026](/blog/dtv-visa-new-requirements-2026). This article stays focused on the funds question alone.

## Personal bank account: the safest, most accepted option

A personal bank account in your own name, holding the full 500,000 THB (or the equivalent in another currency) in liquid funds, is the one answer nobody disputes. Every detailed source on this topic treats it as the reference case, and it's the only category with zero disagreement.

What "liquid" means in practice: money you could withdraw or transfer today, sitting in a normal savings or checking account, not tied up in a product that penalizes early withdrawal. Applicants are generally expected to show **3 to 6 months of bank statements**, with a recent statement pulled close to the application date, a pattern confirmed by [Siam Legal International's DTV guide](https://www.siam-legal.com/thailand-visa/dtv-visa-thailand.php).

**Fixed or time deposits** can also count, but only if the funds stay accessible at any point, not locked into a term you can't break. That distinction matters more than the label on the account: a "savings account" that quietly restricts withdrawals defeats the purpose just as much as an account with the wrong name on it.

## Stocks, brokerage accounts, and investments: it's complicated

Stocks, ETFs, and brokerage accounts are treated as **not accepted** for DTV proof of funds, and I'd rather say that plainly than pretend there's no disagreement online. According to [Siam Legal International](https://www.siam-legal.com/thailand-visa/dtv-visa-thailand.php), a Thailand-based immigration law firm, stock or investment portfolios sit alongside cryptocurrency, pension funds, and retirement accounts as assets "commonly rejected" for the 500,000 THB requirement, consulted 2026-07-03.

One page out there disagrees, listing stock portfolios, mutual funds, and bonds as accepted categories. It doesn't cite a source for that claim, and it's a lone voice against a licensed immigration law firm saying the reverse. On something tied to whether your visa gets approved, that's not a gap I'd gamble on.

The practical approach: if a meaningful share of your 500,000 THB sits in stocks or a brokerage account, convert enough of it to cash in a personal account well before you apply, not the week of. That sidesteps the disagreement entirely instead of hoping your embassy lands on the more generous reading.

## Cryptocurrency: not accepted as proof of funds

**Cryptocurrency** does not count as proof of funds for the DTV visa. Out of everything covered in this article, this is the one point where the sources actually agree instead of splitting.

According to [Siam Legal International](https://www.siam-legal.com/thailand-visa/dtv-visa-thailand.php), a Thailand-based immigration law firm: "Thai Embassies require proof of liquid assets, meaning cash held in a savings or current account that is immediately withdrawable. Cryptocurrency, stocks, and other investment portfolios are not accepted." A separate DTV preparation guide aimed at applicants puts the crypto point even more bluntly: not Bitcoin, not stablecoins, not screenshots from an exchange account.

If crypto makes up part of your funds, the fix is the same as with stocks: convert it to fiat currency and move it into a personal bank account with enough time before your application that it doesn't look like a last-minute deposit. That last part matters just as much as the conversion itself, since a fresh, unexplained deposit raises its own questions (more on that below).

## Can you combine multiple bank accounts?

You can generally combine funds across **multiple bank accounts** to reach the 500,000 THB threshold, as long as every account is clearly in your own name and the total is documented consistently. And honestly, this is the biggest hole in what's out there: people ask this question constantly, and I couldn't find a single editorial guide that answers it head on.

What I have seen is a fairly consistent pattern from applicant discussions and community threads. Spreading funds across two or three accounts at different banks, each with its own statement history, doesn't seem to disqualify anyone on its own. The thread that matters is documentation: each account traceable to you, with a history that matches, rather than one large account plus a handful of small, unexplained ones.

Since no official guidance spells this out directly, treat it as a sensible default rather than a guaranteed rule. If your funds sit across several accounts, a quick check with your specific embassy beats assuming. ## Borrowed or recently transferred funds: a red flag

A large, recent transfer into your account, right before you apply, can draw scrutiny even if the total is correct. This is sometimes called **funds seasoning**, and it's a real pattern rather than a theoretical worry. Embassies have tightened this specifically: [Siam Legal International notes](https://www.siam-legal.com/thailand-visa/dtv-visa-thailand.php) that scrutiny has increased in 2026, with proof now often required that the balance was maintained for at least 3 months before the application, not just present on the day. One preparation guide aimed at applicants goes further with a specific case: an application flagged after **600,000 THB** landed in the account too close to the submission date, well above the required threshold.

The underlying concern is simple: an embassy reviewing your file wants to see funds that are genuinely yours and reasonably settled, not money that appeared the week before you needed it to appear. A same-day loan deposited specifically to pass the threshold reads very differently from savings that have been sitting there for months.

The practical fix costs nothing but time: let funds sit in your account for a few months before applying, rather than transferring everything the day before you submit. If your situation involves a loan or a large one-off transfer and you're not sure how it will look, walking through your specific file with someone who reviews these regularly beats guessing. ## DTV proof of funds vs the cash check at the airport

These are two different checks, and people mix them up all the time. The 500,000 THB is what the embassy looks at when it decides whether to issue your DTV. The second check happens later, at the immigration counter when you land, and it applies to every foreign arrival, not only DTV holders.

Under a Ministry of Interior rule dating back to 1980 (amounts last updated in 2000), an immigration officer can ask you to show you have enough money for your stay. The Tourism Authority of Thailand reminded travellers of it on 6 July 2026, then again with the new visa exemption rules of 15 September 2026, and stressed that it isn't a new measure. [The amounts reported with that reminder](https://visasnews.com/en/thailand-reminds-travelers-to-be-ready-to-show-proof-of-funds-on-arrival/):

| Type of entry | Per person | Per family |
|---|---|---|
| Visa on arrival, transit visa, certain exemptions | 10,000 THB | 20,000 THB |
| Tourist visa, non-immigrant visa | 20,000 THB | 40,000 THB |

Children under 12 are exempt. Checks are random, but refusing entry to a traveller who can't show the funds is legal under Section 12(2) of the Immigration Act, for "having no appropriate means of living".

The reminder doesn't name the DTV specifically, so the cautious reading is to carry the higher figure, 20,000 THB per person. Cash is the form least likely to be questioned; a recent bank statement is a reasonable back-up, but acceptance of anything other than cash is at the officer's discretion. If you are coming visa-exempt first, to try the camp before committing to a DTV, the same check applies to you, and since 15 September 2026 that entry only gives you 30 days ([how the new 30-day rule changes the maths](/blog/dtv-vs-tourist-visa-runs-cost)).

## FAQ on the DTV visa proof of funds

### Can I use a stock portfolio as proof of funds for the DTV visa?

Generally, no. Thailand immigration lawyers list stock and investment portfolios as commonly rejected for the 500,000 THB requirement, though one less-documented source online claims otherwise. The safer approach is converting a sufficient amount to cash in a personal bank account before applying.

### Does cryptocurrency count toward the DTV visa's 500,000 THB requirement?

No. Cryptocurrency, including Bitcoin and stablecoins, is explicitly excluded as proof of funds, confirmed by a Thailand immigration law firm. Convert crypto holdings to fiat currency in a personal account with time to spare before your application.

### Can I combine multiple bank accounts to reach the 500,000 THB threshold?

It appears to be commonly accepted in practice, as long as each account is in your own name with a consistent, traceable history. No official source confirms this directly, so it's worth verifying with your specific embassy if a large share of your funds is split this way.

### Is it a problem if I just transferred money into my account before applying?

It can be. A large, recent transfer right before an application has been linked to added scrutiny in at least one documented case. Funds that have sat in your account for a few months read as more genuine than a deposit made specifically for the application.

### Do I need to show money when I land in Thailand with a DTV?

You might. The 500,000 THB is checked by the embassy before your visa is issued; the airport check is a separate, random one that applies to all foreign arrivals. It asks for 10,000 to 20,000 THB per person depending on the type of entry, and the prudent choice with a DTV is to have 20,000 THB available to show.

## Conclusion

A personal bank account with 500,000 THB in liquid funds is the one answer nobody disputes for DTV visa proof of funds. Stocks and crypto are far less settled, and the safest move with either is converting to cash in your own account well before you apply, rather than hoping your embassy takes the more generous reading.
