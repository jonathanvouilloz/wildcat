January is close, and a lot of people are typing the same question into a search bar. Will Muay Thai make me lose weight? We hear it at Wildcat all the time, from people about to book a flight and from people standing in the garden after their first class.

The honest answer has two parts. **Muay Thai for weight loss** works, but not because of some special calorie furnace. It works because training this hard, this often, changes your habits. This guide gives you the numbers with their sources, what a month at a Thai camp really changes, and how to eat. It does not promise you a number of kilos, because nobody can.

*This article is general information, not medical advice. If you have a health condition, take medication, are pregnant or have a history of disordered eating, talk to a doctor or a registered dietitian before you change how you train or eat.*

## Does Muay Thai help you lose weight? The direct answer

Yes, with a condition. **Whether Muay Thai is good for weight loss** depends on two things: how regularly you train, and whether you eat a little less energy than you use over time. The NHS puts it simply: you lose weight when you take in fewer calories than your body uses. Training raises the "uses" side. Food controls the "takes in" side.

Muay Thai is a good tool for the first half. A session mixes rounds on the pads, skipping, conditioning, kicks and knees, all in the heat. It is hard, it is varied, and most people find it more fun than a treadmill. That last point matters more than it sounds. The best plan is the one you keep doing.

It is not magic, though. You can train five times a week and see little change if you eat far more afterwards. Muscle can also build while fat goes down, so the scale is a blunt tool. Your clothes, your energy and how you move are often better signs.

## How many calories a session really burns

Let us start with what is solid. The Compendium of Physical Activities assigns each activity a MET value, which is a multiple of the energy you burn at rest. The rough formula is: calories burned = MET × body weight in kg × hours.

In the current Compendium (2024 update, same values as in 2011), "martial arts, different types, moderate pace", which names Muay Thai boxing, is listed at 10.3 METs. "Martial arts, slower pace, novice performers, practice" is listed at 5.3 METs. Those are the two ends we can source.

Here is what that means for a Wildcat group session, which lasts about 1.5 hours:

| Body weight | Easy pace (5.3 METs) | Moderate pace (10.3 METs) |
|---|---|---|
| 60 kg | about 480 kcal | about 930 kcal |
| 70 kg | about 560 kcal | about 1,080 kcal |
| 80 kg | about 640 kcal | about 1,240 kcal |

These are estimates for the time you spend actually moving at that pace. A real class includes a warm-up, water breaks and someone explaining the next drill, so most people sit somewhere in the middle, and often nearer the lower end while learning.

This is why the "1,000 calories an hour" claims on gym websites deserve caution. In the table above, only the moderate column for a heavier person, held non-stop, gets close, and that number covers 1.5 hours, not one. The formula itself shows why the numbers move so much: a heavier person burns more than a lighter one doing the same work. Hard sparring burns more than a technique drill. No calculator, including ours, can tell you your exact number.

A useful habit: treat any single calorie figure as a range, and ignore the number your watch shows to the last digit. Watches guess too.

## What a month at a Thai camp does to your body

People often ask us about a weight loss camp in Thailand. We are a family-run Muay Thai camp, not a diet programme, and we prefer to say so. What we can describe is what changes when you swap your normal routine for a month of camp life.

- **Volume.** At Wildcat, group sessions run at 09:00 to 10:30, 15:30 to 17:00, 17:00 to 18:30 and 18:30 to 20:00. Training once or twice a day, most days, is a big jump from two gym visits a week.
- **Movement all day.** You walk, cycle, stretch and stand more than you did at a desk.
- **Sleep and rhythm.** Early nights and a fixed schedule help many people. Sleep is part of how your body recovers and manages appetite.
- **Heat.** Training in Thailand's heat makes you sweat a lot. Sweat is mostly water, so a temporary drop on the scale after a session is not fat loss. Drink and it comes back. If you are picking your dates, see [the best time of year to train in Thailand](/blog/best-time-train-muay-thai-thailand).

So the honest summary is that you tend to lose weight at camp mainly because you change your life, not because Muay Thai does something unique. Some people lose noticeably, some lose little and gain muscle, and some barely change because they eat and drink more than they realise. We will not show you before-and-after results as proof, because a good photo says nothing about the next person.

As a reference point, NHS guidance suggests that losing 0.5 to 1 kg a week is a healthy, steady pace. That is a general guide for people who want to lose weight, not a target we set and not something we predict for you. If you are considering a big change, ask a health professional first.

## Eating in Thailand without undoing the training

Because meals are not part of that 28,000 THB, what you eat is up to you. That is a good thing for weight loss, since you choose. It is also the part that undoes many people's month.

There is no secret here. The general principles are the same as anywhere:

- **A modest deficit, not a crash diet.** The NHS advises against losing weight suddenly with diets. Training hard on very little food leaves you tired, sore and more likely to get hurt.
- **Protein at most meals.** It helps you keep muscle while you lose fat. Thai food makes this easy: grilled chicken, fish, eggs, larb, tofu.
- **Watch the liquids.** Iced coffee with condensed milk, sugary smoothies, soft drinks and beer add up quickly and rarely feel like a meal.
- **Rice is fine.** You are training hard. Simply notice how large the portion is.
- **Hydrate properly.** In this heat, plain water through the day matters. If you sweat heavily in every session, ask a professional about electrolytes.

Some dishes are lighter and some are heavier, but none is banned. A tom yum, a plate of grilled chicken with sticky rice or a papaya salad sits easily next to training. Fried rice with extra oil, rich curries with coconut milk and deep-fried snacks are delicious and worth eating, just not every day. Eating well on the street is cheap, and so is eating badly. ## Weight loss vs making weight: not the same thing

You may have seen fighters "cutting weight" before a fight, sometimes by many kilos in a few days. That is a different activity from losing weight, and you should not copy it.

Making weight means reaching a class limit for a weigh-in. Many athletes do it by restricting food and fluids, sweating in saunas or wearing sweat suits, then rehydrating before they fight. A 2019 review of weight cutting in combat sports (Barley, Chapman and Abbiss) lists these methods, notes how widespread the practice is, and points to "a wide range of potential physical and psychological health risks" with surprisingly little research behind current habits. It also found that large cuts (about 5% of body mass in under 24 hours) impair repeated-effort performance.

For a beginner, this has one practical message. If a diet plan built for a fighter's weigh-in is doing the rounds online, it is not a weight loss plan. Skip it. The sensible route is the slow one: train regularly, eat enough, sleep, and let the change build over weeks.

## 2, 4 or 12 weeks: realistic expectations

We will not put kilos next to durations, because we would be inventing them. Here is what we can say honestly about how it tends to feel.

**Around 2 weeks.** Mostly adaptation. You are sore, hungry, and learning the movements. Your fitness usually feels better before your body looks different. Early scale changes are often water.

**Around 4 weeks.** Your routine is set. Sessions feel easier, your technique improves, and many people notice how they sleep, move and carry themselves. Whether the scale moves depends a lot on food.

**Around 12 weeks.** This is where changes tend to be clearer, if you keep the habits. It is also where a good camp becomes something you would do at home too, which matters more than any month.

## FAQ

### Is Muay Thai good for women who want to lose weight?

Yes, it is a good fit, for the same reasons as for anyone else. Read more in [Muay Thai for women](/blog/muay-thai-for-women).

### Is Muay Thai better than boxing for weight loss?

Both are demanding, and neither has a proven advantage that fits everyone. Muay Thai adds kicks, knees and clinch, so it uses more of the body. The best choice is the one you enjoy enough to keep doing.

### Can Muay Thai help with fat loss without losing muscle?

It can support it, especially with enough protein and rest. Exact results vary from person to person, so we cannot give a figure.

### Will I gain weight or muscle?

Some people do gain muscle, especially in their legs and shoulders, while losing fat. That can keep the scale still even while your body changes. If your appetite jumps too, you may gain weight overall. Both are normal.

### What about Reddit stories of dramatic transformations?

Individual stories are real, but they are individual. They often include changes in diet, sleep and stress that never make the headline. Use them as inspiration, not as a forecast.

## Conclusion

Muay Thai can be a very good way to lose weight, and a month at a camp gives you the routine to make it happen. The training is only half of it, though. Food, sleep and time do the rest, and the kilos vary from person to person. If someone promises you a number, be cautious.
