Before serious training starts, something quiet sometimes happens at the ring at Wildcat. A student kneels facing one corner, palms pressed together, head lowered, while a coach hums a few bars of an old melody. Nobody rushes. That scene is **wai kru ram muay**, and it happens at gyms across Thailand every day, not only before a professional fight.

Most explanations online are either a dry encyclopedia summary or a gym blog post with a stock photo of a headband. This one is neither. I'm Meaw, and I run Wildcat with my family here in Chiang Mai. Our coaches perform this ritual and teach it to every student, fighter or not. Here's what wai kru ram muay actually means, what happens during it, and what to know the first time you see or take part in it.

## What Wai Kru Ram Muay Actually Means

Wai kru ram muay is the ceremony a Muay Thai fighter performs before a fight, and in many camps, before ordinary training too. It isn't a warm-up or decoration. It's how a fighter pays respect to their teacher and lineage before doing anything else.

Break the name into its parts and the meaning becomes clear fast. Wai is the traditional Thai gesture of respect, palms pressed together, seen everywhere in Thai life, not only at a Muay Thai ring. Khru is the Thai form of the Sanskrit word guru, meaning teacher. Ram is the Thai word for dancing in classical style. Muay is boxing. Put together, according to [Wikipedia's entry on the ritual](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wai_khru_ram_muay), **what wai khru ram muay means** translates roughly as "war-dance saluting the teacher," an entire ritual built around **the wai in muay thai**, stretched into several minutes of dance before the first strike is thrown.

## The Two Parts: Wai Kru and Ram Muay

The ceremony has two distinct halves, though most visitors experience them as one continuous performance. Wai kru is the respect-paying part: the bow, the kneeling, the acknowledgment of the teacher and lineage. Ram muay is the dance that follows: respect, then dance, then fight.

### Sealing the Ring

Before the dance itself, a fighter often walks the ring corner to corner, kneeling briefly at each one and touching the ropes. This part is commonly called **sealing the ring**, and it belongs to the wai kru half rather than the ram muay dance. The idea is to close off the space and settle it before what's about to happen inside it. No music cue, no announcer moment, just a fighter alone, moving quietly around a space they're about to fight in.

### Is It Religious, or Is It Respect?

People often ask whether the ritual is religious, and the honest answer is that it depends who you ask. Fighters traditionally bow at each corner in salutation to Buddha, Dharma, and the Sangha of monks, a Buddhist gesture, and the ceremony as a whole is rooted in respect toward teachers, parents, and the people who shaped the sport before them, per the [same Wikipedia entry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wai_khru_ram_muay). Wildcat's coaches teach it that way too: not as a required prayer, but as gratitude toward the people who taught you. Nobody here expects a student who isn't Buddhist, or isn't religious at all, to treat the wai kru as worship. What's expected is respect, for the teacher in front of you and the tradition you've been handed.

## The Ram Muay Dance: Movements and Meaning

Once the respect-paying half ends, **the wai kru dance** begins: slow, circular steps and symbolic gestures performed solo around the ring, timed to live music. No two fighters move through it identically, but most share recognizable pieces: a circling walk, an arm extended in a slow arc, a moment of kneeling, a stance that echoes a drawn bow.

The idea behind **the movements of the wai kru** is symbolic rather than literal. Some describe an arm sweep as recalling a bow and arrow, a nod to old stories connecting fighters and hunters. Others read it as an extension of the wai itself, slower and fuller, performed for the whole room. Which meaning applies usually depends on who taught it to you.

### Every Camp Has Its Own Style

Do all Muay Thai fighters have their own ram muay? Largely, yes. The dance passes down from a kru to their students, camp by camp, rather than being standardized nationally, which is why real variation shows up from one gym to the next.

This has real historical roots. Fighters from **Lopburi, Korat, and Chaiya** were honored as the era's best at a royal tournament in the early 1900s, and their hometown styles were later codified as distinct regional schools, per [Wikipedia's history of Muay Thai](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muay_Thai#20th_century). That same variation carries into the ram muay: [Evolve MMA notes](https://evolve-mma.com/blog/understanding-the-wai-kru-ram-muay/) that each camp typically has its own style of the dance, with subtle differences by region and teacher. At Wildcat, our coaches teach their own version the way it was taught to them, not one fixed choreography lifted from a textbook.

### The Mongkhon and Pra Jiad: What Fighters Wear

During the ceremony, fighters wear two sacred items: the **mongkhon**, a braided headband, and the pra jiad, an armband tied around the bicep. Both are treated as meaningful, not decorative, and both leave the fighter at different moments: the mongkhon comes off right before the first strike, the pra jiad usually stays on. We go much deeper on both in [our full guide to the mongkhon headband](/blog/muay-thai-headband-mongkhon).

## The Sarama Music That Drives It

The ram muay is performed live, set to **ram muay music** played by a small band at ringside. The core instruments are the pi muay, a Thai reed oboe with a piercing tone, alongside hand drums and small cymbals, an ensemble often called sarama.

**The music that plays during wai kru ram muay** isn't background noise. It sets the tempo of the dance, speeding up or slowing down as the fighter moves, and an experienced performer can time every step to it without visibly counting a beat. It's one of the more striking parts of a fight night, before either fighter has thrown a single strike.

## Who Becomes a Kru

A kru is more than a coach. The word describes a formal teacher, someone trained and trusted enough by their own teacher to pass Muay Thai on to the next generation. **Becoming a certified kru** usually means years of training under an established teacher, followed by that teacher's endorsement, not a weekend certificate.

That's why wai kru ram muay matters beyond fight night. Students at Wildcat perform a version of it during ordinary training too, because the gesture is about the relationship with the teacher in front of you. [Meet our coaches](/about/coaches) to see who carries that role at Wildcat day to day.

## What to Expect If You're New to This

If you're about to see or perform your first wai kru ram muay, here's the short version: watch quietly, don't interrupt, and don't worry about getting the movements perfect if a coach asks you to try it.

A few practical points worth knowing beforehand:

- **You don't need to be a fighter.** Many students learn a simplified wai kru as part of ordinary training, long before any fight is on the calendar.
- **Nobody expects religious belief.** Respect for your teacher is what matters, not a specific faith.
- **It's fine to just watch.** If you're trying [a beginner class](/classes/beginners), watching someone else's wai kru quietly is completely normal, not rude.
- **Ask your coach if curious.** Every kru teaches it slightly differently, and most are glad to explain why.

This is part of why Wildcat stays a family camp, not a hardcore fight-only gym: a beginner gets the same respectful introduction as anyone training toward a fight. Check [our class schedule](/classes) and come watch a session; no booking needed just to observe.

As Meaw Boonpradub, who runs Wildcat with her family, puts it: "New students sometimes think the wai kru is only for real fighters, something they'll grow into later. It isn't. It's just how you say thank you to the person teaching you, and that starts on day one."

## FAQ on the Wai Kru Ram Muay Ceremony

### What does Wai Kru Ram Muay mean?

Wai means respect, khru means teacher, ram means dance, and muay means boxing. Together, the phrase describes a fighter paying respect to their teacher, followed by a ritual dance.

### What is the difference between Wai Kru and Ram Muay?

Wai kru is the respect-paying half: bowing, kneeling, acknowledging the teacher and lineage. Ram muay is the dance that follows, performed around the ring before the fight begins.

### Why do Muay Thai fighters dance before a fight?

The dance honors the people who taught them, and it settles the mind and body before the fight itself arrives.

### Is the Wai Kru ritual religious?

It includes a Buddhist salutation at each corner of the ring, but it isn't mandatory worship. Most camps, including Wildcat, frame it as respect toward teachers and lineage first.

### Do all Muay Thai fighters have their own Ram Muay?

Largely, yes. The dance passes down from a kru to their students rather than being standardized nationally, so movements and rhythm vary from one camp or region to the next.

### Do I have to perform the Wai Kru as a beginner?

No. Beginners can simply watch at first. Many later learn a simplified version as part of normal training, at their own pace.
