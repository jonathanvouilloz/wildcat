Yes, you can **fight Muay Thai in Thailand** as a foreigner, and many visitors do. A Muay Thai fight in Thailand is not something you book online like a class, though. In practice the gym or your trainer decides if you are ready, finds a date and an opponent, and puts you on a card. This guide covers how it usually works, what level people ask for, how weight classes read, what you can earn, and where the risks sit: injury, insurance and your visa. Where we have no first-hand knowledge, we name the source instead.

## Can a foreigner fight Muay Thai in Thailand? The short answer and how a first fight is booked

Yes. Foreigners fight in Thailand's stadiums and on tourist-friendly shows, and a gym or trainer usually arranges it for them. The big Bangkok stadiums, Lumpinee and Rajadamnern, are not a starting point: [a Bangkok gym](https://www.kstmuaythai.com/?p=1771) says it takes years of fighting to get there. Most first fights happen on smaller local cards. A bout under [the WMC rulebook](https://muaythai2000.com/en/pages/rule.php) runs up to five rounds of three minutes with two minutes of rest, and elbows are legal.

### How a foreigner actually gets a first fight

You do not book the bout yourself. A Bangkok gym [wrote in 2017](https://www.kstmuaythai.com/?p=1771) that getting a fight scheduled is "a straightforward task" for your trainer or gym owner. [A gym on Koh Samui](https://punchitgym.com/muay-thai-fights-for-foreigners-in-thailand/) says it gets requests from foreign visitors every week, and that it only puts people in the ring who can represent the gym well. The path usually looks like this:

1. You train regularly, ideally for months before you arrive.
2. The coach watches you work and decides if you are ready.
3. The gym proposes a date and an opponent at a similar weight.
4. You go through the weigh-in and a medical check.
5. You fight.

How long this takes depends on the camp. The Koh Samui gym says its fight preparation takes at least three weeks and ideally four, and [another page from the same gym](https://punchitgym.com/muay-thai-professional-fighter-as-a-foreigner-in-thailand/) says someone who has trained for a long time but never fought needs about two to three months to be fit and good enough. Those are one gym's numbers, not a rule. Ask the coach who would train you.

## Are you ready? Level, format and weight classes

A coach's opinion beats any checklist you read online, including this one.

### Level, training volume and who gets refused

Many **fight camps in Thailand** that publish requirements ask for a base before they will match you. The Koh Samui gym lists these before you think about your first Muay Thai fight in Thailand:

- three to four Muay Thai or kickboxing sessions per week over several months;
- two or three runs of four to five kilometres per week;
- an amateur fight at home, if you can.

The same gym says it does not support fighters over 40 without solid experience, because the health risk is too high. [Sumalee Boxing Gym](https://sumaleeboxinggym.com/muay-thai-fight-in-thailand-10-tips-to-prepare/) describes a fight build-up of about 20 hours a week (three to four hours a day, six days a week) for at least eight weeks, with sparring every second or third day. 

None of this guarantees you will or will not be accepted: it depends on the coach, the opponents available and how you train. If you are not sure, ask your coach first. If you are at the start, [a first class at Wildcat](/classes/beginners) is a calmer place to begin, and [the Fight Team at Wildcat](/classes) is the programme for members who are already ready to spar and compete.

### Format and weight classes: what the table really means

The WMC rulebook, written by one sanctioning organisation and not Thai law, sets five rounds of three minutes with two minutes of rest. Before the first round both fighters perform the wai kru ritual, which you can read about in [the wai kru and ram muay](/blog/wai-kru-ram-muay-explained), and [the mongkhon headband](/blog/muay-thai-headband-mongkhon) is part of the same tradition.

Weight classes in the same rulebook are listed as maximums. These are some of them:

| Class | Not exceeding |
|---|---|
| Flyweight | 112 lb (50.8 kg) |
| Bantamweight | 118 lb (53.5 kg) |
| Featherweight | 126 lb (57.2 kg) |
| Lightweight | 135 lb (61.2 kg) |
| Welterweight | 147 lb (66.6 kg) |
| Middleweight | 160 lb (71.6 kg) |
| Light heavyweight | 175 lb (79.4 kg) |

The rulebook lists more classes on both sides. The caveat: the same class name can cover quite different weights from one promotion to the next, so confirm the exact limit with the promoter before weigh-in day. Weigh-in timing also varies. [One guide](https://muaythaicitizen.com/muay-thai-weight-classes/) says it is usually the day before or the morning of the fight, which leaves roughly 12 to 24 hours to rehydrate. The WMC rulebook itself says weigh-in happens on the day of the fight, at least three hours before the bout. A missed weight can mean a cancelled fight, or a bout that goes ahead if promoter and opponent agree.

We will not give you a weight-cut plan. Cutting weight carries real dehydration risks, so settle it with your coach and a doctor.

## Money, safety and visa: what can go wrong

Three things trip people up: expecting to be paid, assuming a policy covers them, and not checking their visa. They are reasons to ask questions early.

### What you actually get paid

Not much. The Koh Samui gym [writes](https://punchitgym.com/muay-thai-professional-fighter-as-a-foreigner-in-thailand/) that living from fighting is hardly possible for 90% of foreign fighters. At gyms that mostly train fighters, it says, you may train for free but the purse is split 50/50 with the gym, so you take home about 3,000 to 5,000 THB per fight in the first months, and as a rule you cannot fight more than once a month. That is one gym's figure, not ours. [A profile](https://www.muaythaicitizen.com/fighting-on-holidays/) of a woman fighter who moved to Chiang Mai says she was paid 3,000 THB on average per fight, including title bouts, and that it was not enough to live on. A gym owner in north-east Thailand who also manages fighters [says](https://www.thailandstarterkit.com/health/ama-recap-frances-watthana/) only a select number of foreigners make a living from fighting, usually sponsored by their gym. A Bangkok gym says Lumpinee and Rajadamnern pay 10 to 15 times more than other promotions, but it takes years to get there.

So treat a first fight as an experience, not income. For context, [the Fighter program price](/classes#pricing) at Wildcat is 7,000 THB for one month, and [what Muay Thai training costs in Thailand](/blog/muay-thai-training-thailand-cost) shows how that compares with other camps.

### Safety: medical check, rest periods and insurance

[The WMC rulebook](https://muaythai2000.com/en/pages/rule.php) says a ringside physician checks every fighter before weigh-in, stays at ringside through the event and examines fighters after the bout, setting medical rest periods. Those run from 7 days up to 30 days after a knockout loss. The referee's main duty is to protect the fighters. A foreign fighter [writing on his blog](https://8limbsus.com/?p=5475) says getting your blood pressure taken by the doctor on arrival is the norm. We found no official Thai document that sets out medical rules for foreign fighters, and [the Boxing Act of 1999](https://muaythai2000.com/en/pages/laws.php) that governs the sport did not give us an answer on that point either. The WMC rulebook is one organisation's standard, not a guarantee for every show.

Insurance is where visitors often get caught. A forum member on [an 8 Limbs thread](https://8limbsus.com/muay-thai-forum/topic/1268-fighting-in-thailand-insurance/) warns that a lot of insurers exclude martial arts, so read the rules very well. [One review](https://www.thailandstarterkit.com/health/world-nomads-review/) of a popular travel policy says Muay Thai training needs its higher-tier plan, and nothing we read confirms that a bout itself is covered. Before you fly, ask your insurer in writing whether a fight is covered, and keep the reply.

### Visa and work permit: tourist, DTV, ED and a paid fight

Here the sources disagree, and we are not going to pick a winner.

- One position says there is no work-permit category for professional fighters, that the situation is a grey area, and that most fighters simply keep a tourist visa valid. A gym owner in north-east Thailand said this in [an interview](https://www.thailandstarterkit.com/health/ama-recap-frances-watthana/), adding that some gyms can offer education visas.
- The other position, from [a visa agency](https://www.issacompass.com/professions/thailand-visa-for-professional-athletes), says that if you earn income from fighting for Thai companies you cannot use the DTV, and would need a Non-B visa plus a work permit through a sponsoring Thai organisation that meets capital and staffing requirements. That is a commercial source, and we found no official page confirming it.

What the sources do agree on is that [the DTV covers Muay Thai training](https://thethaiger.com/visas/dtv/) with an enrolment letter, 500,000 THB in funds and stays of up to 180 days per entry. Whether an unpaid bout, a bout with expenses covered or a paid fight changes your status is the question nobody answered clearly. Check with the Thai embassy that handles your application, with Thai immigration or with a lawyer before you accept money to fight, and remember that rules change. For the training side, read [Education Visa vs DTV for Muay Thai](/blog/ed-visa-vs-dtv-muay-thai), [which visa fits your training length](/blog/thailand-visa-guide-by-training-length) and [DTV, ED and tourist visa compared](/dtv-visa#compare).

## FAQ: fighting Muay Thai in Thailand

### Can a foreigner fight Muay Thai in Thailand?

Yes. Foreigners fight in Thailand, usually on cards that a gym or trainer arranges. Expect the coach to judge your level first. Lumpinee and Rajadamnern are not an entry point.

### How much do foreigners get paid to fight Muay Thai in Thailand?

Very little at first: one gym quotes about 3,000 to 5,000 THB per bout, shared 50/50 with the gym. Plan as if the fight pays nothing.

### How long do you need to train before your first fight?

It depends on the coach. One gym asks for several months of regular training plus three to four weeks of fight preparation, another for eight weeks or more at about 20 hours a week.

### Do you need a work permit to fight Muay Thai in Thailand?

Sources conflict. One calls it a grey area with no permit category for fighters, while a visa agency says paid fights call for a Non-B visa and a work permit. Ask the embassy, Thai immigration or a lawyer before accepting any money.

### Is travel insurance valid for a Muay Thai fight?

Not automatically. Many policies exclude martial arts or cover only training on a higher plan. Read the policy, ask the insurer in writing whether a fight is covered, and keep the answer.
