## What actually changed on 15 September 2026

The short version: the visa exemption most travellers used to enter Thailand dropped from 60 days to 30, and a new set of nationalities lost visa-free entry altogether. Neither change touches the DTV or the tourist visa directly, but both change what "just show up and figure it out" costs you.

Thailand's visa exemption now gives 30 days on arrival instead of 60, with one further 30-day extension available at an immigration office. Entries under the exemption through land border checkpoints are capped at two per calendar year, except for Malaysia, Brunei, Indonesia and Singapore. If you were already in the country before 15 September, you keep the stay length you were granted on entry, no adjustment needed. For the full detail, including the separate DTV requirements that landed on 31 August, see [the full rundown of the 31 August and 15 September changes](/blog/dtv-visa-new-requirements-2026).

### The 21 nationalities that now need a visa in advance

According to [visasnews.com](https://visasnews.com/en/thailand-these-21-nationalities-will-need-a-visa-starting-september-15-2026/), 21 nationalities lose visa-free entry from 15 September 2026: Albania, Andorra, Colombia, Cuba, Dominica, the Dominican Republic, Ecuador, Guatemala, Jamaica, Kosovo, Monaco, Morocco, Mexico, Panama, Papua New Guinea, San Marino, Sri Lanka, Tonga, Trinidad and Tobago, Uruguay and Uzbekistan. That report is the source we found for this list, and we have not seen it independently corroborated elsewhere, so treat it as reported rather than as settled fact until you confirm it against your own embassy or the official e-Visa portal.

Most trainees reading this hold a passport from the US, the UK, the EU, Australia or Canada, none of which are on that list. But if you are one of these 21 nationalities, or you are travelling with a training partner who is, the practical step is the same either way: apply for an e-Visa before the flight, not after landing.

### Proof of funds and the TDAC

Two separate requirements sit alongside the exemption reform, neither one new, both worth knowing before you land. First, **proof of funds**: Thai immigration can ask to see 10,000 THB per person (20,000 THB per family) if you are entering under the exemption, a visa on arrival or in transit, or 20,000 THB per person (40,000 THB per family) if you hold a tourist or non-immigrant visa. Children under 12 are exempt. This is not a rule written for the 2026 reform. It dates to a 1980 Ministry of Interior requirement, with the amounts last updated in 2000, and entry can technically be refused under Section 12(2) of the Immigration Act for insufficient means of subsistence. In practice it is rarely enforced at random, but having a printed bank statement or enough cash on you removes the risk entirely. Our full breakdown, including how this interacts with DTV fund requirements, lives in [our DTV proof of funds guide](/blog/dtv-visa-proof-of-funds).

Second, the **TDAC** (Thailand Digital Arrival Card). It has been mandatory since 1 May 2025 for every foreign arrival by air, land or sea, submitted online up to 72 hours before you land, at tdac.immigration.go.th. This one is unrelated to the September reform and has simply become routine, but it still trips up people who have not travelled to Thailand since it launched.

## Choose your Thailand visa by training length

Here is the question that actually matters: how long do you want to train? Everything else, exemption, tourist visa, DTV, ED, falls out of that one answer. Use the table below as a starting point, then read the section for your row.

| Training length | Visa to consider | What to plan for |
|---|---|---|
| Up to 30 days | Visa exemption | Nothing to file in advance. Bring proof of funds and a return or onward ticket. |
| 30 to 60 days | Visa exemption plus one extension | Apply in person at an immigration office once you are here, for a 1,900 THB fee. Not guaranteed. |
| Up to 90 days | Tourist visa (TR) | Apply before you leave home, through the e-Visa portal. One extension of 30 days is built in. |
| 3 to 6 months or more | DTV, or ED as an alternative | Apply from your home country, with roughly 500,000 THB in funds and a training programme that runs at least six months in practice. |

The people who plan around their real training goal from the start have an easier file than the people who arrive on a short stamp and try to convert it once they have already fallen for the camp. The next four sections walk through each row.

## Training on the 30-day exemption

If you are coming for a trial week, a short training camp, or you genuinely are not sure yet whether Chiang Mai is for you, the exemption covers it without any paperwork before you fly. Since 15 September 2026, it gives 30 days on arrival, down from the previous 60. One extension of up to 30 more days is possible at a Thai immigration office, for a 1,900 THB fee, but it is granted at the discretion of the officer handling your file, not automatically. If you are entering through a land border, you are limited to two exemption entries per calendar year, except from Malaysia, Brunei, Indonesia or Singapore. If you already entered before 15 September, none of this changes what you were granted on arrival.

For most people testing the water, 30 to 60 days is plenty to try classes, get a feel for the camp, and decide whether to come back for longer. If that is your plan, [drop-in class pricing](/classes#pricing) covers what a short stay actually costs on the training side.

## The 90-day route: a Thailand tourist visa (TR)

If you already know, before you book the flight, that you want two to three months of training, the tourist visa gives you that block without relying on a discretionary extension. It is applied for before departure, through the e-Visa system, and gives 60 days on arrival, extendable once by 30 more, for a maximum of 90 days.

The key difference from the exemption is timing. The exemption is granted at the border, on arrival, with no application. The tourist visa has to be requested and approved before you travel, which means building the extra week or two of processing time into your planning. For a trainee who has already decided on a two to three month block, a fighter camp season, a specific event to build toward, this route removes the uncertainty of a discretionary extension and gives you the full 90 days from the outset.

## For 3 to 6 months or more: the DTV, or the ED as an alternative

Once you are looking at three months or more, the exemption and the tourist visa stop being realistic options, and the conversation shifts to the DTV. Muay Thai qualifies as a soft power activity under the DTV, and nothing about the eligibility criteria or the cost structure changed with this reform. We are not going to repeat the full breakdown here. See [the full DTV visa guide](/dtv-visa) for the complete picture, or go straight to [check your DTV eligibility](/dtv-visa/eligibility) if you already know your situation. If you want the numbers side by side against staying on repeated tourist entries, [the DTV vs tourist visa runs cost comparison](/blog/dtv-vs-tourist-visa-runs-cost) has that worked out.

The ED visa is the other route worth knowing about for a long stay, built around enrolment at a registered school rather than a soft power activity. It is not the route we would point most Muay Thai trainees toward, but it exists, and [the ED visa vs DTV comparison for Muay Thai training](/blog/ed-visa-vs-dtv-muay-thai) lays out where each one fits.

Whichever of the two you go for, both are applied for from your home country, before you travel. Neither one converts cleanly from a tourist stamp you are already standing on in Chiang Mai. If a six-month or longer stay is even a possibility, the honest move is to start that file before you book the flight, not after you have already fallen for the training. ## FAQ on choosing a Thailand visa by training length

### Do I need a visa at all for a short Muay Thai trip?

Not for most nationalities. If you are eligible for the visa exemption, a trip of up to 30 days needs no visa application at all, just a passport, proof of funds and a return or onward ticket. You only need to think about a visa once you want to stay longer than the exemption, with or without its extension, allows.

### What happens if I overstay my 30-day exemption?

You owe a fine for every day past your permitted stay, and immigration handles the exact consequences case by case. A short overstay is usually resolved on the way out at the airport, but a longer one can mean detention or a future entry ban. If you think you will run past your allowed days, apply for the extension in advance or leave and re-enter cleanly rather than letting the date pass.

### Can I extend my visa exemption more than once?

No. The exemption allows one extension of up to 30 days, for a 1,900 THB fee, and it is granted at the discretion of the immigration officer reviewing your file. Once that extension runs out, you need a different visa category to stay longer, or you need to leave the country.

### Is the 90-day tourist visa the same as the DTV?

No, they solve different problems. The **tourist visa (TR)** tops out at 90 days including its extension, and it is meant for a defined short trip. The DTV runs five years, with 180 days per entry and one 180-day extension, and it is built around a longer commitment such as Muay Thai training as a soft power activity. If your training block is under three months, the tourist visa is simpler. Past that, the DTV is the route that actually fits.

### Does the 30-day rule change anything for an existing DTV holder?

No. The 15 September 2026 change affects visa-free entry only. If you already hold a DTV, you still get 180 days per entry and the option of one 180-day extension, handled through Thai Immigration, entirely separate from the exemption rules.

### What if my nationality is not on the exemption list?

Then you need to apply for a visa in advance regardless of how long you plan to stay, exemption timelines do not apply to you at all. The clearest way to check is the official e-Visa portal or your nearest Thai embassy, since exemption eligibility varies by nationality and can change, as the 21 nationalities losing it from 15 September 2026 show.

### Do I need to show proof of funds even on a short trip?

Yes, in principle, regardless of trip length. Immigration can ask to see 10,000 THB per person under the exemption, or 20,000 THB per person if you hold a tourist or non-immigrant visa, with double those amounts for a family and an exemption for children under 12. It is rarely checked at random, but it is a real requirement, not a formality that disappeared, so carrying proof costs you nothing and removes the risk entirely.

## Conclusion

The maths changed on 15 September 2026, but the decision underneath it did not. Work out how long you actually want to train first, then pick the exemption, the tourist visa, or the DTV based on that number, not the other way around. Anyone applying for the DTV or the ED still needs to start that file from home, before the flight, not from a tourist stamp already inside Thailand.
